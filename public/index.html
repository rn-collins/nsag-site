The Framework

The 16 Core Principles

These sixteen principles are the operating logic of Nervous-System-Aware Governance — the foundational commitments that shape how NSAG approaches every advisory engagement, every assessment dimension, and every governance recommendation. They were developed across ten years of neuroscience research, clinical observation, and institutional analysis.

I — Foundation

What NSAG claims is true before any intervention.

1
Institutions regulate nervous system states — always. Every institutional encounter produces a physiological response. This regulation is biological. It happens whether the institution intends it or not.
2
The loop runs both directions. Institutions shape behavior. Behavior shapes outcomes. Outcomes reshape institutions. Intervention is possible at every point.
3
Every institution is already governed — the question is by what. Default governance is governance by historical precedent, cognitive bias, and the interests of whoever built the original system.

II — Mechanisms

How institutions produce their effects.

4
Language is the first governance layer. Institutional language activates cognitive and physiological responses before any procedure, environment, or policy takes effect.
5
The built environment is governance. Physical space, acoustics, lighting, and nature access determine what is cognitively and physiologically possible within an institution.
6
Digital environments produce real nervous system effects. Cognitive load, trust erosion, decision fatigue, and attention fragmentation are design problems, not user problems.
7
Threat and safety are institutional products. They are produced by design: language, environment, power structure, procedure, architecture.
8
Cognitive load is a design variable, not a user deficiency. When people fail to navigate systems, the system has often created the conditions for that failure.
9
Chronic stress produces institutional incapacity. Burnout, error, attrition, and ethical failure are institutional design outputs.

III — The Social Layer

How humans navigate systems with each other.

10
Relationships and social capital are physiological infrastructure. Belonging, trust, and human connection directly regulate nervous system states and determine who can access what within systems.
11
Trust is the primary medium of institutional exchange. Before compliance, participation, learning, healing, or disclosure — there is trust.
12
Identity formation is an institutional product. Institutions regulate who people become, not only what they do.
13
Agency is a design variable. Institutions that reduce the agency of the people they serve impair the precise states required for the outcomes they claim to want.

IV — The Governance Imperative

What follows from accepting the above.

14
Governance precedes intentional design. Without a framework specifying what good design means, institutions default to efficiency and compliance.
15
What institutions cannot see, they cannot govern. The disclosure gap, the measurement gap, the visibility gap — these are governance failures before they are individual failures.
16
Evidence without governance is inert. Research accumulates. Governance is what connects evidence to decision-making and makes it a design requirement.

About

A Framework That Started Freshman Year

NSAG did not begin as a framework. It began as a question that first appeared at the University of Pennsylvania — and kept reappearing in every domain that came after.

The question: how do institutions evaluate evidence, protect people, and govern emerging technologies when public trust and commercial pressure are in tension?

NSAG is the attempt to operationalize that question into a governance framework with measurable outcomes.

University of Pennsylvania

Work-study appointment, first semester: Penn Brain Center, Cognitive Neuroscience Laboratory. The question was already forming. Eighteen months later: Penn Medicine Neuropsychiatry — internship with Ruben C. Gur and Raquel E. Gur, among the world’s leading neuroimaging researchers. Then: Penn Bioethics Center — where the question moved from neuroscience to governance. Then: medical device safety and FDA recall research with the National Research Center for Women & Families. The evidence governance gap named itself: why do institutions fail to act on evidence they already have?

Shanghai, China

Education consultant. Studies in developmental psychology applied to cross-cultural institutional communication. The question of how institutions shape the people they govern — through language, learning environment, and the assumptions built into their design.

Boston University School of Medicine

M.S. in Anatomy & Neurobiology. Researcher in the Brain Plasticity & Neuroimaging Laboratory (Schon Lab) — where the thesis question was also the lab’s primary research program: how racialized stress shapes the medial temporal lobe memory system in older adults. Graduate anatomy instructor and prosector. Volunteered to spend July in the anatomy lab dissecting with only Gray’s Anatomy — the book, not the show — for backup. The thesis named what the body requires of institutional design. That question became NSAG.

Harvard Business School

Medical researcher. Organizational decision-making, institutional behavior, and implementation science. The gap between evidence and what institutions do with it — approached from the organizational side.

Yale Program for Psychedelic Science

Regulatory and compliance: FDA, DEA, IRB. Comparative legal research on psychedelic-assisted therapy access across 30+ international jurisdictions (with Kathryn Tucker JD, end-of-life PAT). The governance gap for emerging therapeutics becomes visible at scale. What governance framework do institutions need to adopt evidence responsibly — before harm occurs, not after?

Northeastern University School of Law · NSAG Founded

JD Candidate, Northeastern University School of Law. Seven law clerk positions across psychedelics, cannabis, AI governance, and biophilic design. The question finally found its institutional form. NSAG is the framework.

Local Intelligence

The Hawaiʻi Advantage

Honolulu is a living laboratory for every NSAG module. Hawaiʻi's regulatory environment, indigenous practices, and demographic profile create deployment conditions unavailable elsewhere in the United States.

Indigenous Governance Case Study · M7 · Conscious Cities

The Hawaiian practice of talk story — informal community conversation as civic participation — is a pre-existing nervous-system-aware governance mechanism. It governs through relationship, storytelling, and shared physical presence rather than procedural formality. Before NSAG named the framework, Hawaiʻi was already practicing it.

M1 · M2 · Legal Infrastructure

Hawaiʻi's state court system, Legal Aid Society, and University of Hawaiʻi William S. Richardson School of Law create a contained legal ecosystem for testing trauma-informed and AI-navigated legal design interventions at manageable scale.

M4 · M9 · Cannabis & Healthcare

Hawaiʻi's medical cannabis program (operational since 2015), its federally qualified health centers serving Native Hawaiian and Pacific Islander populations, and active legislative development on psychedelics create an active policy laboratory for M4 and M9.

M5 · M7 · Biophilic & Civic

Near-equatorial light, ocean views, and year-round indoor-outdoor conditions make Hawaiʻi among the world's most evidence-rich environments for biophilic design research. The climate's year-round indoor-outdoor access creates real-world testing conditions for every M5 assessment dimension that are unavailable in mainland climates.

M10 · M11 · Healthcare Architecture

Hawaiʻi's geographically isolated hospital system, its unique relationship between military healthcare (TRICARE at Tripler), and a concentrated urban healthcare corridor in Honolulu create accessible conditions for M10 healthcare built environment research.

M12 · Education Systems

Hawaiʻi's single statewide school district, its multilingual learner population, and Richardson School of Law's community-focused legal education mission create a replicable case environment for M12 education system research.

M8 · Burnout Recovery Infrastructure

Hawaiʻi's nurse vacancy rate and physician shortage create acute burnout conditions across the healthcare system. Geographic isolation limits recovery pathways and amplifies institutional stress, making Honolulu a high-stakes deployment environment for M8 organizational design interventions.
